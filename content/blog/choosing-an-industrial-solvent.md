Choose a solvent by working through five questions in order: does it dissolve what you need, how fast does it evaporate, how flammable is it, does it mix with water, and what health and regulatory limits come with it. The first question rules most candidates out. The others decide between the few that are left, and the last one increasingly decides the outcome.

## Does it dissolve what you need?

"Like dissolves like" is still the best first rule. Polar materials dissolve in polar solvents; non-polar materials dissolve in non-polar ones.

- **Alcohols** such as [methanol](/products/methanol) and [isopropyl alcohol](/products/isopropyl-alcohol) are polar and form hydrogen bonds. They dissolve many resins, dyes and polar organics, and they mix with water.
- **Esters** such as [ethyl acetate](/products/ethyl-acetate) sit in the middle. They dissolve nitrocellulose, many acrylics and adhesives, which is why they are common in inks, coatings and flexible packaging.
- **Aromatics** such as [toluene](/products/toluene) are strong solvents for many resins, rubbers and paints.
- **Chlorinated solvents** such as [methylene chloride](/products/methylene-chloride) dissolve a very wide range of organics and are used in extraction and paint stripping.
- **Alkanes** such as [n-heptane and hexane](/products/n-heptane-hexane) are non-polar. They dissolve oils and fats but not much else, which makes them selective, for example in edible oil extraction.

For a precise match, formulators use solubility parameters such as Hansen parameters, which split solvency into dispersion, polar and hydrogen-bonding parts. A solvent close to the solute on all three usually dissolves it. A practical lab test with your own material is still the final word.

## How fast should it evaporate?

Evaporation rate controls drying time, film quality and how much solvent you lose. Boiling point gives a quick, rough guide: lower boiling solvents usually evaporate faster.

| Solvent | Boiling point | Flash point | Water miscibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Methylene chloride | about 40 °C | none under standard tests | Slight |
| Methanol | about 65 °C | about 11 °C | Complete |
| n-Hexane | about 69 °C | about −22 °C | Practically none |
| Ethyl acetate | about 77 °C | about −4 °C | Partial (about 8%) |
| Isopropyl alcohol | about 82 °C | about 12 °C | Complete |
| n-Heptane | about 98 °C | about −4 °C | Practically none |
| Toluene | about 111 °C | about 4 °C | Practically none |

Values are approximate figures for the pure substances. Check the SDS for the grade you buy.

Too fast, and a coating can blush, skin over or trap bubbles; too slow, and production lines wait. Blends are common for exactly this reason: a fast solvent for early drying with a slower one to let the film level.

## How flammable is it?

Most organic solvents are highly flammable, and the flash point tells you how easily the vapour ignites. Every solvent in the table except methylene chloride has a flash point below normal room temperature, which means vapour above an open container can ignite at room temperature.

That affects everything from storage to electrical equipment. Flammable solvents need bonding and earthing to prevent static discharge, ventilation to keep vapour below its lower explosive limit, and storage that follows local fire regulations.

Methylene chloride is the notable exception. It has no flash point under standard tests, which is one reason it became popular. It still decomposes in fire to toxic gases, and its health hazards are significant, so non-flammable does not mean low-risk.

## Does it need to mix with water?

Water miscibility affects cleaning, extraction and waste handling.

- **Fully miscible solvents** like methanol and IPA are useful when water is part of the process, for example in cleaning, disinfection or as a co-solvent. They are harder to separate from aqueous waste.
- **Immiscible solvents** like toluene, heptane and hexane separate from water into a second layer. That makes them useful for liquid-liquid extraction and easier to recover.
- **Partly miscible solvents** like ethyl acetate and methylene chloride are common in extraction too, but some solvent dissolves in the water phase, so plan for losses and treatment.

Density matters in extraction as well. Methylene chloride is denser than water and forms the bottom layer; the hydrocarbons and ethyl acetate float on top.

## What are the health and regulatory limits?

This question often decides the final choice. Each solvent has its own hazard profile, and workplace exposure limits differ between countries.

- **Methanol** is toxic if swallowed, inhaled or absorbed through the skin. It can cause blindness and death, so it needs careful labelling and control.
- **Methylene chloride** is a suspected carcinogen, and the body converts it to carbon monoxide. Several markets restrict its use, notably in consumer paint strippers.
- **n-Hexane** can damage peripheral nerves with long exposure. This is one reason heptane is often preferred where it performs well enough.
- **Toluene** can affect the nervous system and is suspected of harming the unborn child.
- **Ethyl acetate** and **IPA** have comparatively lower toxicity, though both irritate eyes and cause drowsiness at high vapour concentrations.

Solvent emissions are also regulated as volatile organic compounds in many regions. If your product is sold into markets with VOC limits, the solvent choice may be fixed by the rule rather than by performance.

## Can it be recovered and reused?

Distillation recovers many solvents for reuse and cuts both cost and emissions. A single-component solvent with a clear boiling point is easiest to recover. Blends, azeotropes and water-miscible solvents are harder. Ethyl acetate, for example, forms an azeotrope with water, and IPA forms one too, which complicates drying the recovered solvent.

Think about recovery at the selection stage, not after the plant is built.

## How do you put it together?

A short process works for most cases:

1. List what the solvent must dissolve and test the candidates on your material.
2. Set the evaporation window your process can accept.
3. Rule out anything your site cannot store or handle safely.
4. Check water compatibility against your process and waste route.
5. Check exposure limits and market regulations where your product will be sold.
6. Consider recovery and total cost, not just price per litre.
